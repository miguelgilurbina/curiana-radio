3.3 The Falcón-Lara Highlands Sphere

The Falcón-Lara Highland Sphere is defined geologically by the Falcón Anticline, an
orographic system of sierras and mountains arising south of the western coast of Falcón.
These series of parallel ranges are oriented in a southwest to northeast axis (Fig. 28).
South of the coastal plains the Sierras de Montafitas are found toward the west ending in
the eastern lowlands of Maracaibo, where the slightly more north-northeast oriented
Sierra de Jirajaras arise. This system is interrupted by the Urumaco depression, which
continues broadening to the south: the Pedergal Basin. East of the Pedregal-Pecaya
Basin the Sierra de San Luis de Curimagua, rises abruptly. In this part of Falcón, the
coastal ranges abut the coastal zone just east of Coro, where they break down into a
series of hills and smaller ranges, effectively dividing the semiarid western coast from
the subhumid eastern coastal plains (Fig. 34).

South of the Sierra de San Luis, there is a broad valley that drains the Hueque and
Ricoa rivers. This valley is called La Cruz de Tara-tara, and is characterized by
secondary growth vegetation and grasslands (Figs. 37, 38, 39). This valley opens and
widens as one moves northeast along the Hueque-Ricoa rivers. Both rivers discharge
into the Caribbean Sea (Matteucci and Colma 1982, Matteucci et al. 1979, COPLANARH
1975c [#44]: 16-ff.).

South of the La Cruz de Tara-tara valley and the Pedregal-Pecaya Basin, another
orographic system emerges. The Sierra of Siruma (a Guajiro noun that glosses as
'cloud') arises. Along with the Sierra de Jirajara, it forms the massifs that define the
limits of the eastern Maracaibo Basin. South and parallel to the ranges of the coast,
Lomas del Caballo (north) and the Baragua Highlands (south) arise. South of these
highlands the valley of Carora is found.159 South of the Carora depression, one finds the
Sierra of Barbacoa and the northernmost extension of the Venezuelan Andes (Cordillera
of Mérida and of Portuguesa), which are a distinct entity and not considered here as part
of the Flacón-Lara Sphere. Continuing east-northeast of the Baragua Highlands, the
Buena Vista and Churuguara cordilleras arise, including the El Copeyal, El Buco and
Morrocoy sierras east of Churuguara (Figs. 34, 38). South of the Churuguara ranges
and east of the Baragua range, the highlands of Matarere and Bobare emerge (Fig. 38).

This set of ranges in the north and the Portuguesa Highlands in the south surround
the Barquisimeto-Quibor plateau/valley, a lowland valley which is not considered part
of this sphere for cultural as well as geographical reasons. The last highland system to
be considered here is the Sierra de Aroa, east of the Sierra de Bobare. And east of Aroa
one finds the Sierra of Nirgúa which is already part of the Caribbean Andes (Cordillera
de la Costa, etc.). Aroa and Nirgiia are separated by the Yaracuy River valley that
connects the Caribbean coast of Venezuela with the Llanos and Barquisimeto Valley. Both
Aroa and Nirgua are geologically different than the Venezuelan Andes and the Falcón-Lara
orographic systems (COPLANARH 1975c [#44]: 80).

Travel across these mountains (e.g. between Coro and Barquisimeto) in the XVIth
century was hazardous and difficult, however, several expeditions beginning with
Federmann (1530-1531), Hohermuth (or Espira [1535-1538]), Hutten (1541-
1546), and others had used the highland routes to reach Barquisimeto. Strategical
lowland, or relatively low elevation routes of communication between different areas
ought to be mentioned briefly. The Táchira Depression, near the modern city of San
Cristóbal provides an effective bridge from the Northwestern Llanos to the southern
Maracaibo Basin, avoiding the 3,000 to 4,000 meter highlands of Mérida. The major
drawback, for the Spanish (they brought mules and horses), was the swamps of the
southern Maracaibo lowlands, which made it difficult to travel.

The other important communication bridge is found between the Sierra de Siruma
and the Venezuelan Andes, at the head waters of the Motatán river. Although not strictly
lowlands, there are a series of passages that allow easier communication between the
Sabana de Carora Depression and the eastern Maracaibo lowlands. Hence, one could
travel from Barquisimeto through Quíbor and Sabana de Carora (all plains) into the
Maracaibo Basin with comparatively more ease than attempting to do so across the
Trujillo and Mérida Highlands.

Finally, the third strategic cross-roads is the gap between the Portuguesa and the
Nirgúa highlands. Through this gap there is an effective lowland communication between
the Northern Llanos (Cojedes River) and the Barquisimeto Valley as well as the Yaracuy
Valley. The latter, running between Sierras of Aroa and Nirgúa, opens to the Caribbean
Sea. Indeed, this was the only route used by the Spanish to penetrate the Llanos from
Coro (the exception is navigating from Trinidad through the Orinoco Delta and west along
Apure or Meta rivers).

The Jirajaran "family" consists of three closely related languages: Jirajara, Ayoman
(Ayaman) and Gayón (Coyón, Cuyón). A quick comparison with Proto-Chibchan suggests
few points of comparison, although the vocabularies of Jirajaran are very limited.16
However, Greenberg (1987: 106-ff., 382) has presented linguistic evidence that
Suggests that Jirajara belongs in in the Paezan sub-division of the Macro-Chibchan-
Paezan division of the Amerindian "super" Stock. In this sub-division, Jirajara is
placed along with Nuclear Paezan (Andoquí, Barabacoa, Chocó and Páez), Allantiac, Betoi,
Chimú, lonama, Mura, Timucua (Florida, U.S.) and Warrau (Orinoco). For the moment
| will only say that | have serious doubts as to the Paezan affiliation of Timucua and
Warrau, and of Yanéama's affiliation to the Chibchan sub-division, and prefer to
maintain all three as separate clusters.

The Jirajaran include several ethnic groups; namely, the Jirajara (or Girara,
Jirara, Xidehara), the Gayón (Coyón, Cuyón), and the Ayoman (Ayoman). The latter, as
we shall see, were further differentiated into two sub-groups based on physical
(genetic) attributes: the "midget" Ayomanes and the "mixed" (with Jirajara)
Ayomanes.

The Jirajara in the XVIth century where reported in the Sierra de San Luis,
immediately south of Coro (Fig. 38), in the Nirgtia mountains, across the Sierra of Aroa
and the Yaracuy Valley (Fig. 39) and in the Sierra of Jirajara of western Falcón (Arcaya
1977: 37).

3.4 Coastal_Falcón Sphere

The Caquetío are among the most widespread Arawakan-speaking groups in norterhn
South America in the XVIth century. As Arcaya (1 977) first noted in the 1920's, the
Caquetío peoples were found in the coastal plains of what is today Falcón State, between

the islands of Aruba, Curacao, and Bonaire, and the Peninsula of Paraguana. A second

as | will show later, the Caquetio were not a single homogeneous polity as
anthropological literature portrays it (Steward and Faron 1959, Sanoja and Vargas
1979). Nevertheless, they were historically bound together by a common language and a
common origin.

3.4.1 The Landscape of Coastal Falcón

Obviously, each one of these regions inhabited by Caquetio presented different
ecological conditions, resources, and strategies of adaptation. The one thing all these
areas have in common is that they are are tropical lowland environments, and that the

Caquetío's primary mode of subsistence was agriculture.

Coastal Falcón is primarily a semi-arid xerophitic province, where agriculture is
difficult to sustain, although not impossible.The riverine systems along the western
coast of Falcón are all characterized by an intermittent water flow (e.g. Capatarida,
Zazárida, Mitare rivers). A succesful harvest in this area requires crops with short
growing seasons to coincide with the brief rainy seasons, or otherwise it needs human
intervention in the form of irrigation by artificial ditches, or "hand" irrigation by
extracting rainwater collected from artificial ponds (jaguey). But this quasi-
equilibrium of carefully managed agriculture (that had to prevail in pre- and proto-
historic times) in semi-arid conditions was subjected to pressures which caused a
geometric increase in the acceleration of erosional processes that devastated the
potential for the soils to retain moisture. As Juan de Castellanos himself noted (and he
was an ocular witness), coastal Falcón was:

3.5 A_Historical Synthesis of the Conquest of the Caquetio

The Caquetio of Coastal Falcón formed the largest and the most complex of all Caquetio
polities. It is of no surprise that the Spanish, early on, attempted to capitalize on the
more hierarchical nature of centralized authority (in the paramount chief Manaure) to
accomplish their tasks of population dominance with ease. Controlling Manaure, they
though, was to control all of his subjects. This pattern of Spanish conquest by
controlling a “Head of State" and, therefore, in one stroke being able to wield power to all
his/her subjects was a well tried tactic, already successfully applied in the process of
the Reconquista against the Moors, as well as in the conquest of the Canaries Islands. In
the New World this tactic was only successfull where societies had a more centralized
power and political/economic administration, and where the concepts of "victory" and
“defeat” in warfare were more similar to those held by the Spanish (e.g. The Inca and the
Aztec Triple Alliance). The Coastal Caquetio appeared to the Spanish to be such a polity,
and in some ways it nearly worked, but, as we shall see, not for too long.
