3.2.3 The Macro-Chibchan-speaking Ethnic Groups
3.2.3.1 Linguistic Background

Still within the boundaries of the Western Sphere, a number of ethnic groups were
described by the XVith century chroniclers. These various ethnic groups were
linguistically bound together in the Macro-Chibchan-Paezan Stock. As with the
Arawakan Stock, the Macro-Chibchan-Paezan is still a "messy" linguistic stock. Th
problem is aggravated when one attempts to correlate XVIth century groups with this
Stock, simply because the linguistic data is so scanty for that time period. Before
entering into details of ethnic relationships, it is useful to discuss the linguistic
relationships first, for these imply historical nexae.

Alva Wheeler (1972: 93-ff.) has analyzed several Macro-Chibchan/Paezan
languages; namely, Kogi (Caggaba), Arhuaco (or Ika, Ica), Marocacero, "Motilén",
Muiska (Chibcha proper), Motilón, and Tunebo. Wheeler determined through
comparative lexicostatistical analysis that the Kogi, Arhuaco, and Marocacero, "form a
sub-group coordinate with Chibcha and Tunebo" with Motilón being the most divergent
within this language set (Wheeler 1972: 94). The Chibchan Motilón Indians are now
recognized as the Barí, and their language is also known as Dobokubí (Wilbert 1961;
Lizarralde and Beckerman 1982). My own comparison between Wheeler's Motilón
(from Iquiricadora, near Río del Oro in Venezuela) and Wilbert's Dobokubi (Sierra of
Perijá, Colombia-Venezuela) suggest very close relationships (Oliver i.p. 1986: 15).
Indeed these may be at the level of dialectical differentiation.

Looking at cognate percentages (Wheeler 1972: 94), one finds that Kogi and Arhuaco
form a close pair (57% [based on Swadesh's 200 word list). This is not surprising
given the historical relationships outlined in the previous section (Arhuaco is also
known as Bintucua or Ica language [Constenla 1981: 72-73]). . The Kogi language is also
