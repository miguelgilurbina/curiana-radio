4.7 Linguistic Correlations

The hypothesized expansion of the Macro-Tocuyanoid Tradition, out of an ancient

polychrome tradition originating in the Central Amazon, into Northwestern Venezuela -

(Fig. 75), to a large measure correlates with the model of the early Proto-Maipuran
expansion that eventually gave rise to the Guajiro and Paraujano languages (and
Hakaritama?). A comparison between the archaeological distribution of Macro-
Tocuyanoid traditions (Fig. 75) and the linguistic model (Figs. 26-27 [in Ch.-3]) show
a remarkable degree of spatial correlation. Furthermore, the proposed
lexicostatistcally determined time of separation between Paraujano and Guajiro (ca. A.D.
950) coincides with a marked stylistic shift and subsistence shift at the site of La Pitia
(Hokomo to Siruma), which | submit to mark the beginnings of the expansion of Pre-
Hispanic Guajiro-Wayú into the marginal territory of the Guajira peninsula; a
territory where terrestrial game hunting and limited agriculture were the predominant
subsistence activities, a cultural pattern that was regarded in high esteem by the
Guajiro-Wayú. The Paraujano group (the Onoto), became the kusi (Kusi'na) or
‘fishermen’, reverting back to their ancestral modus vivendi (Kusu period), trading
their maritime goods for agricultural produce.

The Guajiro language has been estimated to have a temporal depth in excess of 3.7
millennia (Table 8 [in Ch.-2]) when compared to the Newikian groups that expanded to
the Llanos (Piapoco and Achagua); that is, a minimum divergence of 2500-2000 B.C.
This lexicostatistical time of divergence does coincide with my prediction of the
divergence of the Macro-Tocuyanoid into its various constituent traditions. However, |
would have expected that the lexicostatistical date to be closer to the 3000 to 4000 B.C.
time range, to coincide with the reconstructed expansion of the ancient polychrome
tradition into the Orinoco. As one moves farther back in time, lexicostatistically
determined dates seem to fall short of expectations. If anything, my 80% retention rate
is probably too fast a rate of vocabulary change, and it is conceivable that a much more
conservative 85-90% retention rate is more reflective of "real" calendric time --at
least in comparison to the archaeological model.

4.9 The Macro-Dabajuroid Hierarchical Classification

The Macro-Dabajuroid Tradition is comprised of two major ceramic traditions,
Dabajuroid and Tierroid, both of which are characterized by a predominantly rectilinear
polychrome and bichrome painted style, which sharply contrasts with the preceding
Macro-Tocuyanoid Tradition.

The Macro-Dabajuroid Tradition, especially the Tierran and Dabajuran sub-
traditions, is characterized by a sharp dichotomy of the pottery into two very different
kinds of wares, one of which is more crudely made and whose shapes are functionally
related to cooking and storage (Appendix C: Fig. C-35 to C-44; Fig. 95 a and 95 b in
text), and another much better made finer ware, which appears to be functionally
related to food-serving and storage (mostly liquids, but in some Sub-traditions, to
"store" the bone bundles of the deceased [Appendix C: Figs. C-45 to C-58). This shape,
function, and ware dichotomy, however, becomes less clearly established as one proceeds
from the two core Sub-traditions (Tierran and Dabajuran) to their cognate sub-
traditions, whose styles are generally simpler (e.g. Capachoan sub-tradition). While
the finer wares are almost invariably associated to bichrome and polychrome paint

4.10 The Spatial Distribution of the Macro-Dabajuroid Tradition

As shown in Figure 76, the Macro-Dabajuroid has a widespread geographical
distribution. The Dabajuroid Tradition, in particular extends throughout most of coastal
Venezuela. The Dabajuran Sub-tradition has as its core area the entire coast of Falcón
State (Fig. 77), including the peninsula of Paraguaná (Fig. 78), and the islands of
Aruba, Curacao, and Bonaire. Clearly these archaeological boundaries of the
Bachqueroan, Dabajuran and Tierran sub-traditions correlate quite well with the
ethnohistorical Coastal and Barqusisimeto-Yaracuy Caquetio polities (cf. Chapter 3).
The Mirindayan sub-tradition of Trujillo's Highlands are not, however, correlated to
Caquetio (or any Arawakan-speaking groups).

Essentially, the so-called Dabajuro style (Cruxent and Rouse 1958: 71-74), has
been redefined into three different complexes: Túcua, Early and Late Urumaco, and
Los Médanos A and B. Of these, the Early and Late Urumaco complexes, partially
encompass the original definition of Dabajuro Style proposed by Cruxent and Rouse
(1958). In fact, the Urumaco complex is identical to Dabajuro style minus the
inclusion of the Maticora (Malamboid) stylistic features.

The formal description by Cruxent and Rouse, including Cruxent's original
description (1955) of Dabajuro style, includes a ceramic complex, which it is
demonstrably a Tortolitan (Malamboid) development (Oliver Ms.1987b); namely, the
incised style of Maticora, which Cruxent obtained from Casas Viejas site near the town of
Dabajuro (Fig. 77: FAL-47). In the original 1955 publication, Cruxent maintained
Casas Viejas as a separate collection from the "classic Dabajuro" ceramics obtained from
the same locality (my Urumaco complex), but in the joint monograph with Rouse
(1958) this distinction became blurred.

4.11 The Non-Dabajuroid Complexes of Coastal Falcón

Within the State of Falcón (Fig. 77), the great majority of pottery-bearing sites
belong to the Dabajuran Sub-tradition (except for the Historic "Creole" sites). There
are only a few exceptions; site belonging to the Maticora and Tortolitan Sub-tradition,
and the "isolated" complexes of Mauroa, Coy-Coy, and Guacoa. | will briefly discuss this
only to point out their relative temporal position (if known) and their spatial
distribution.

4.11.1 The Matícora Complexes (Malamboid Tradition).

One such non-Dabajuroid complex has already been discussed above in connection to
the Matícora complex sites (Fig. 77: FAL-7 to FAL-1 0). These sites are all of the
Malamboid Tradition, and included in the Tortolitan Sub-tradition.

between A.D. 1 and A.D. 1300/1400, since a few Maticora style incised sherds have

The FAL-100 site (Fig. 82) has been divided into various sectors of controlled
surface collections; namely, sectors 1 through 6 (Fig. 82). All of the ceramics are
found within five centimeters of the surface. Since the fine sands are constantly in
motion due to the strong Trade Winds, there is no “stratigraphy" to speak of. The pottery
is easily collected by raking each of the surface units after careful handpicking. Several
test pits were placed in the site, but did not contribute to the diachronic assessment of
the site. These cuts were helpful for geomorphological studies in the area, which will be
the subject of another study.

Three C14 assays were obtained. Sample ISGS-1253 was collected from a test pit
within Sector-1, and yielded a date of 570 + 70 B.P., or cal. A.D. 1 299-[1363]-1424.
The second sample was collected from a Stone-ringed hearth, which was partly exposed
to the surface, north of Sector-2. This hearth is associated with pottery features
characteristic of Sector-1. This sample (ISGS-1423) yielded a date of 500 + 110
B.P., which calibrates to A.D. 1280-[1407]-1440. The third sample (ISGS-1424)
yielded a date of 330 + 110 B.P., which calibrates to A.D. 1521-[1572]-1641
(Stuiver and Reimer 1986 [my Fig. 85, Table 15)).

The first pair of dates are associated to what | designate as Los Médanos complex,
phase A, while the historic date is associated with the assemblage in Sector-2 (from a
stone ringed hearth). The historic date is confirmed by two fragments of XVIth century
Spanish Olive Jar rims (same pot), whose profile is unmistakably of an early XVith
century type, as defined by Goggin (1960). I have a strong hunch, however, that the
earlier sigma of the historic date (A.D. 1521) is more probable than the median or
upper upper sigma, considering the Spanish events that took place in Coro after 1528
(cf. Ch.-3). The A.D. 1520 assemblage is quite unique in that it has been strongly
affected by outside influences (namely, the Portacelli complex out of Colombia). The
associated ceramic assemblage in Sector-2 is designated as Los Médanos complex, phase
B.

4.13 The Portacelli Connection

The changes that took place in site FAL-100 (also FAL-101 shows the same
pattern), within the sand dunes of Coro, are of utmost importance. lt can be
demonstrated beyond any doubt that Los Médanos-A complex had developed out of a Late
Urumaco complex. All of its Ordinary Ware cooking ollas, griddles, hemispherical
bowls, etc., are diagnostic shapes of Late Urumaco. It also has the same set of bulbar and
shafted bases that are typical of Urumaco complex bowls. But, in addition, there is a
sudden appearance of significant amounts of a Black-on-Red painted, very well made,
almost burnished ware pertaining to small bowl (15-18 cms.. diam..), some of which
had rim forms unknown for Late Urumaco. These bowls are always, without exception,
supported by simple annular bases, almost never showing any perforation. Shafted and
Bulbar bases, as well as other forms of leg support (tripods, leg-ring, etc) are never
associated to Black on Red painted bowls. The designs associated with these bowls were
also new features not found previously. Most diagnostic of these designs is the
zoomorphic motif of what appears to be a bird. The exact same design (Fig. 89-90) is
found only in Portacelli (the Tierra de Los Indios "birds" are of a different style and
executed on different vessel shapes).

The Dabajuran branch (Fig. 96: #1-4, 5) continued expanding and colonizing
eastern Falcón, reaching the area of Dabajuro by about A.D. 800 (Tucua complex). By
A.D. 1100 to 1200 the Dabajuran (Earl Urumaco) had colonized much of Eastern Falcón
(still poorly known archaeologically), Coro, Paraguana, and had settled along major
coastal rivers in western Falcón between Borojó and Mitare rivers. At this time the
Dabajuran (Urumaco) had also learned maritime navigation, and had colonized the
Netherlands Antilles (Fig. 96: #6). The Falconian Dabajuran Sub-tradition flourished,
and continued expanding. The evidence of FAL-100 site, as discussed above, indicates
that by at least A.D. 1300 the Dabajuran peoples had sailed as far as the west coast of the
Guajira (Fig. 96: 48) and had contacted peoples of the Portacelli complex, an exchange
which locally affected the pottery of the Dabajuran group inhabiting the sand dune sites
of the Médanos de Coro. This local development continued until after Spanish contact, to
the middle of the XVIth century. Meanwhile, at about the same time frame (Fig. 96: #4
to 7) a late, simpler, Dabajuran development (regressive, stylistically) spread further
west, beyond into the Maracaibo Basin, reaching the region sometime between A.D. 1350
and 1500. This later expansion evolving out of the Dabajuran complex designated as Late
Urumaco, had diverged (simplification) enough to consider it as the beginnings of
another Sub-tradition, the Bachaqueroan, which was cut short by the Spanish conquest.

CHAPTER 5

Towards an Integrated Model of Arawakan Expansion

Thus far, in Chapter 2 (section 2.10) | have presented a three stage model of
expansion mainly based on linguistic evidence. In Chapter 4, | presented a model of
Macro-Tocuyanoid (sections 4.5 and 4.6) and Macro-Dabajuroid (section 4.15)
expansion based on archaeological evidence. This following section integrates both
models, offering a holistic view of the spread of Arawakan-speaking peoples and their
cultural traditions into Northwestern Venezuela and Northeastern Colombia.

The careful reader may note a few minor discrepancies between the details of the
foregoing model and those mentioned for each independent data set in the previous
chapters. This reflects the natural process of growth as | analyzed and considered the
corpus of evidence throughout this thesis. Rather than regarding these as
“contradictions” or perhaps as "inconsistencies", | prefer to regard these “variations” as
the result of bringing together the archaeological, linguistic and ethnohistorical models
under a single, coherent model. After comparing all of three patterns of data, | have
found it useful to modify --however slightly-- some of my earlier propositions. It also
serves to emphasize that there is still room for alternative interpretations on the basis
of the evidence presented throughout my thesis

my hypotheses to test, whether in any part or all of the data sets involved. Linguists are

5.1 Proto-Arawakan-Maipuran Expansion, Stage 1

(+4000 to 2200 B.C.)

By at least 4000 B.C. a hypothesized Proto-Arawakan/Maipuran and Proto-Tupian
(e.g. Proto-Equatorial) group occupied the middle Amazon floodplains as far east as the
Nhamudá-Trombetas affluents (Stage 3 of Brochado's modified model [cf. Ch-2]). The
Proto-Arawakan/Maipuran branch expanded northwards into the Upper Rio Negro
Basin, and down the Orinoco. This expansion involved the spread of what | have been
tentatively calling as “Ancient” Amazonian Polychrome Tradition, from which | postulate
the emergence of the Macro-Tocuyanoid Tradition.

By about 3700 B.C. one representative prehistoric complex of this expansion is
found in the lowest levels of Agúerito site. | propose that by the time this branch
reached the Orinoco, the differentiation between Proto-Arawakan and my Eariy Proto-
Maipuran had already taken place.315

5.2 Early Proto-Maipuran Expansion. Stage 2a

(2200-1500 B.C.)

At some time prior to 1500 B.C., but after 2200 B.C (Fig. 74), out of the Early
Proto-Maipuran groups spreading into the Orinoco valley, a new line of development was
to become historically known as the Guajiro and Paraujano languages. We might call this
line as "Pre-Guajiroan". The "Pre-Guajiroan” peoples carried with them what 1
hypothesize to be the ancestral tradition of the Macro-Tocuyanoid Tradition, which was
to develop as they migrated westward into the Llanos of Venezuela and Colombia.
