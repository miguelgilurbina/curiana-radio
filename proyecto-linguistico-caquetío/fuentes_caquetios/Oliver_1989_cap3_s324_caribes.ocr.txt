3.2.4 The Carib-Speaking Ethnic Groups
3.2.4.1_Linguistic Background

The most recent synthesis of Carib language classifications, has proposed two main
divisions: Northern Carib and Southern Carib (Durbin 1977). The Northern Carib
division is organized into five major sub-groupings: a) Coastal Carib, b) Western
Guayana Carib'45, c) Galibi, d) East-West Guayana Carib, and e) Northern Brasilian
Carib.146 Durbin (1977: 35) is quite confident that the Northern Carib are closer to
Proto-Carib than any of the Southern Carib languages are. The most important aspect of
Durbin's analysis and conclusions is that all the Carib languages recorded within the
Western Sphere (Magdalena, César, Rancheria, and Maracaibo), are much closer to the
Carib languages spoken along much of the central to eastern coast of Venezuela and parts
of the northern Llanos, than to any of the Carib languages of the Guayana.

The scenario, as | have reconstructed it, portrays a process of territorial attrition
for the Arawakan-speaking groups (excluding the Caquetio) and the territorial
predominance of the Carib- and Chibchan-speaking ethnic groups. The Arawakan-
speaking groups were circumscribed to the northernmost confines of the Guajira
peninsula; they were completely surrounded by other ethnic groups. On the other hand,
the Carib-speaking groups, judging by the wide territorial distribution, were
numerically superior and had established control over most (if not all) the prime
lowland-agricultural soils; consequently, their settlements were larger than those
described for Arawakan -speaking groups such as the Onoto. The Chibchan-speaking
groups , more than the Carib-speaking groups, were highland oriented (e.g. Sierra
Nevada, Highlands of Santander, the southern Perija range).

It is conceivable that the process of displacing the Arawakan-speaking groups into
marginal territories (i.e. Guajira peninsula) was the result of a Strong competition for
prime agricultural lands. Both, Arawakan and Carib-speaking groups throughout South
America are characterized as Tropical Forest Cultures (cf. Lathrap 1970, 1977) and,
as such, both groups in the Western Sphere had vied for lowland floodplain control.
Carib and Arawakan spatial distributions contrast sharply with the XVith century
Macro-Chibchan-speaking groups. With the exception of the Coanao and some of the
Aruago, the settlement emphasis is on sierra and highland areas.
